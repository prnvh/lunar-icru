# Do the published lunar-propellant models answer the same question?

## Abstract

Major lunar-propellant studies are often read as if they disagreed about one price. This paper asks a prior question: when the public record is held fixed, can those studies be recomputed, and do they estimate the same decision quantity? Four versioned cases were implemented from public sources. Kornuta's 2018-report net present values, at 10 percent over ten years, keep the published signs, and the rates of return of the same cash flows round to the printed rates. Jones 2020 reproduces plant mass and power at a stated production rate, and its campaign cost ratio stays unresolved. Sowers's NIAC cost and revenue totals reproduce, and the published company internal rates of return do not, because the report states durations without an annual cash-flow table. Charania's 2007 cost-table subtotals match the sums of their rows, and the required sale prices remain outputs of an untabulated zero-NPV calculation that the paper associates with two different weighted-average costs of capital. No second model from this historical sample can be re-run on a quantity commensurable with Kornuta's NPV. Those cases stay in the audit and out of the controlled benchmark. The Part I finding is that the apparent disagreement is, on the public record, a disagreement about questions and a shortage of schedules, not a measured residual after shared assumptions. Part II is specified separately and uses only models that can be rerun.

## 1. Question

The fixed question is: when major lunar-propellant economic models are given the same physical and economic assumptions, do they converge on the same conclusion, and if not, what causes the remaining disagreement?

That question has a gate. Two models can be given the same assumptions only after each can be re-run, and only after they are shown to evaluate the same decision quantity. A different native metric is a different question. An invented cash-flow schedule is not a reconstruction.

The paper is an intercomparison of existing models. It does not propose a new lunar-propellant venture, and it does not estimate a modern launch price.

## 2. What was reconstructed

Each case is one source edition, one question, one native metric, and one system boundary. Equations live in a Python module. Historical inputs live in a separate JSON file. A missing input stays null. Overrides are rejected unless the module already names them. Copyrighted PDFs are not part of the repository. The checks below are regenerated by:

```text
py -3 -m unittest tests/test_reproductions.py
```

Kornuta, Abbud-Madrid, and colleagues, in the 2018 Lunar and Planetary Institute report, ask for the net present value of a mining company that sells propellant on the lunar surface. The implemented case uses a constant annual cost, a ten-year life, and a 10 percent discount rate, with end-of-year cash flows. The directory name `kornuta2019` marks the study family. The journal spreadsheet was not recovered, so this baseline is the report's printed cash-flow description.

Jones and colleagues, AIAA 2020-4041, ask whether cumulative in-situ production cost is below cumulative Earth-delivery cost over a government campaign. Costs are undiscounted FY2019 million dollars. The selected point inside the paper's sweep is ten years at 17 tonnes per year of surface demand, then fourteen years of cislunar demand, a three-year plant life, and an inert-mass fraction of 0.25. That point is a selection, not a unique baseline.

Sowers and colleagues, NIAC 2020, ask for a company's internal rate of return under commercial sales and under public-private cases, and they report a separate NASA-savings view. The static cost and revenue chain is implemented. The annual schedule is not.

Charania and DePasquale, IAC 2007, ask for the propellant sale price that sets net present value to zero. The public paper is the 17-page SEI copy. The financing schedule is not in it.

## 3. Reproduction

Kornuta is the only operational financial baseline. All seven scenario net present values keep the published sign. The Moon-only case is about −$234.22 million, against a displayed −$234 million. Discounting the same flows from the start of each year makes that case positive, so that timing is rejected. The rates of return of the end-of-year flows round to the seven printed rates. The cost year of the report's dollars is unresolved. The printed recurring cost is $129 million per year; the components sum to $128 million and are not used to replace the printed net present values.

Jones 2020, at 17 tonnes per year on the Duke plant, returns a Table 5 mass of 2,262.7 kg and a power of 10.4958 kWe. The campaign ratio is null. The missing pieces are a subsystem mass allocation, a lander sizing convention, a loader count, a choice between Table 5 mass and a 40 kWe reactor count, and an event ledger for development, production, launch, and replacement. The cost-estimating relationships are implemented and are not a campaign. A discontinuity at 15,000 kg is preserved: the published cubic fit is not smoothed.

Sowers's printed revenue branch gives undiscounted company net cash of $2,155.5 million, $3,125.6 million, and $5,046.9 million for the commercial, lunar public-private, and lunar-plus-Mars cases. The published company internal rates of return are 8.84 percent, 15.8 percent, and 15.4 percent. Schedules that stay inside the report's own windows — four years of development and production, an 18-month deployment, a ten-year life, and Mars demand from operating year 3 — produce other rates, including commercial rates of about 8.44 percent, 7.29 percent, and 10.15 percent. Some public-private schedules have more than one sign change, so a single rate is undefined. The 2021 *New Space* article repeats the same rate table and the same cumulative-cash prose. It does not add the years. The default calculated rate in the module is therefore null. Mars printed revenue of $971 million and demand-derived revenue of $941 million are both retained as a source inconsistency.

Charania's Table 2 header figures are subtotals. The component rows sum to them. The required prices, including $26,845/kg, $133,947/kg, and $7,053,265/kg in the cases the paper tabulates, are not recalculated. The paper prints both 21.7 percent and 22.7 percent as the weighted average cost of capital attached to those prices. Equity beta and the market premium are not numeric, so neither rate is recomputed or chosen.

## 4. Why there is no common benchmark

A numerical comparison was allowed only if two independently reconstructed models could evaluate one declared decision quantity without unsupported logic. A recovered cash-flow stream may be discounted at a declared rate. A stream inferred from a reported rate of return may not.

The public search covered the NIAC report, the 2021 article, Shishko's 2019 ICEAA slides, the Charania paper, and the Blair et al. 2002 report and its PDF mirrors. Shishko names cash-flow sheets and does not publish them. Blair prints toolkit output, including Version 5 statements, and describes the toolkit. The Excel file was not found. Printed statements cannot be re-run when demand, price, or launch cost changes, so they are not a second baseline. No Charania financing workbook was found. Pelech, Roesler, and Saydam 2019 has no legal open full text in this search, and no shadow-library copy was used.

The two 2019 Jones papers were checked as their own campaign-cost cases. The lunar-surface paper prices landers and plants with Project Cost Estimating Capability response surfaces and does not print the coefficients. The cis-lunar paper prices elements with that capability and with PRICE-H. It reports commercial Earth delivery at $40,000/kg, SLS delivery at $46,000/kg, and a least expensive lunar case at $78,000/kg. Those figures were not recomputed. Neither paper supplies the 2020 campaign ledger. Bennett and Metzger are not additional votes.

`research/benchmark-schema.json` therefore keeps every benchmark value null.

## 5. What the disagreement is

The studies that can be checked do not share a dependent variable.

Kornuta reports an investor's net present value for surface sales. Jones 2020 reports an undiscounted ratio of government campaign costs, and the ratio cannot be completed from the paper. Sowers reports a company rate of return whose schedule is not in the paper, plus a separate government-savings view. Charania reports a financed sale price whose debt schedule is not in the paper. Placing those headlines on one dollar-per-kilogram axis would answer a question none of the sources asked.

Where a number can be checked, the failure mode is specific. Kornuta's sign is stable under the printed timing and unstable under the rejected timing, which is an accounting convention, not a physical disagreement. Sowers's totals and rates come apart because more than one schedule fits the stated durations. Charania's cost stack checks and the price does not, and the paper itself states two capital costs. Jones's sizing equations check and the campaign does not, because subsystem masses and the event list are absent.

That is the attribution the public record supports: different decision metrics, and missing schedules inside the financial papers. It is not a decomposition of a shared output into input effects, architecture effects, and equation effects. That decomposition waits on a second re-runnable model.

## 6. Limits

The Kornuta baseline is the report's annuity description, not a recovered workbook. A later spreadsheet could move the net present values inside the displayed rounding. Sowers's Mars revenue inconsistency is preserved rather than repaired. Charania's two capital costs are recorded rather than averaged. Figure digitization was not used to invent Sowers's charted cash flows or Jones's breakeven contours. No inflation conversion was applied across unresolved cost years. No modern reusable-launch case was run, because there is no pair of models on which to run it.

## 7. Conclusion

This is Part I. The historical sample was chosen for influence and structural diversity, not because the papers could be rerun. On that public record, Kornuta's net present value can be regenerated. Sowers, Charania, and Jones can be checked in part and cannot regenerate their headlines. Blair supplies printed output without the toolkit. Pelech's full text was not legally available. Published conclusions are easier to find than executable model definitions.

Those cases stay in the study. They do not enter the controlled benchmark. Part II, specified in [benchmark-eligibility.md](benchmark-eligibility.md), uses only models an independent researcher can rerun and then change. A later workbook for Sowers, Shishko, Charania, or Blair, or a lawful Pelech text, can move that historical case into Part II. Another paper that repeats a headline rate or price does not.
